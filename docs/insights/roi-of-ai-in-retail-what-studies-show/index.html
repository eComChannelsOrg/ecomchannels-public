The ROI of AI in Retail: What the Studies Actually Show

What research from IBM, Deloitte, McKinsey and IHL says about AI returns in retail, why many projects stall, and how online sellers can get measurable payback.5 min read6 cited sourcesUpdated 30 October 2026

Key figureOnly 25% of AI initiatives delivered expected ROI, CEOs saySource: newsroom.ibm.com

The research on AI returns in retail says two things at once: the potential is large, and most organisations are not yet capturing it. IBM's 2025 CEO study found only 25% of AI initiatives delivered the expected ROI, and Deloitte found most organisations wait two to four years for a satisfactory return. The projects that pay back fastest are narrow, measurable and tied to workflows that already cost money, such as inventory, listings, order handling and reconciliation.

The size of the prize

The headline estimates are big. McKinsey estimated that generative AI could add US$400 billion to US$660 billion a year to retail and consumer packaged goods, equal to 1.2 to 2.0% of annual revenues, mainly through marketing, sales and customer interactions. Inventory is another large pool: IHL Group puts the global cost of out-of-stocks and overstocks at US$1.73 trillion a year, or 6.5% of global retail sales.

IHL also reports that retailers deploying AI and machine learning achieved sales growth 2.3 times higher and profit growth 2.5 times higher than competitors. Treat that as correlation rather than proof: retailers that invest in AI are often better run in other ways too. Still, it shows where leading retailers are putting effort.

The reality check

Surveys of executives paint a more sober picture.

  • IBM's survey of 2,000 CEOs found only 25% of AI initiatives delivered expected ROI over the past few years, and only 16% had scaled across the enterprise. Half said rapid investment had left them with disconnected, piecemeal technology.
  • Deloitte's 2025 survey of 1,854 executives found most achieved a satisfactory return on a typical AI use case within two to four years, well beyond the seven to 12 months usually expected of technology investments. Only 6% saw payback in under a year.
  • McKinsey's 2025 global survey found 39% of respondents attribute any EBIT impact to AI, and most of those say less than 5% of EBIT comes from AI.

Retail is no exception. When McKinsey surveyed 52 Fortune 500 retail executives in 2024, most were piloting or scaling generative AI, but only two said they had implemented it successfully across their organisations.

Why returns stall

The studies point to recurring causes:

  • Disconnected systems. AI layered on fragmented data produces fragmented results. IBM's finding that half of CEOs now have piecemeal technology is a warning for retailers adding a separate AI tool for every task.
  • No workflow change. McKinsey's "AI high performers", about 6% of respondents, are the ones redesigning workflows and setting growth goals, not just efficiency goals.
  • Hard-to-isolate benefits. Deloitte's interviewees said AI gains often arrive alongside process and team changes, so they are hard to measure separately unless you set a baseline first.
  • Spreading too thin. McKinsey's retail work found successful retailers focus on transforming specific domains rather than running many small experiments.

Where retail AI pays back fastest

For small and mid-sized online retailers, the fastest returns usually come from automating work you already pay for, where the before-and-after is easy to measure:

WorkflowWhat to measure
Listing creation and updatesHours per listing, listing errors, conversion rate
Order consolidation and stock syncOversells, cancellations, order handling time
Shipping labels and trackingDispatch time, late dispatch rate, "where is my order" contacts
Settlement reconciliationHours per month, short payments recovered
Demand and replenishmentStockouts, excess stock, cash tied up in inventory

These are not glamorous, but they share three features that make ROI provable: high volume, clear rules and a cost you can already count. Inventory is covered in more depth in AI demand forecasting for retailers.

How to build a business case that holds up

  1. Baseline first. Record time spent, error rates and costs for one workflow over a normal month.
  2. Choose one domain. Listings, orders or finance, not all three at once.
  3. Prefer integrated tools. Fewer disconnected systems reduces the piecemeal problem IBM describes.
  4. Keep humans approving outputs so errors do not wipe out gains.
  5. Set a realistic payback window. Deloitte's data suggests broad AI programs take years; narrow automation of a single workflow should show results within months, and if it does not, stop or change it.

FAQ

What is a realistic payback period for AI in retail?

Broad, organisation-wide AI programs often take two to four years according to Deloitte's 2025 survey. Narrow automation of a single high-volume workflow, such as listings or reconciliation, should show measurable time savings much sooner if it is working.

Why do so many AI projects fail to deliver ROI?

Common reasons in the research include disconnected data and systems, adding AI without changing the workflow, and not measuring a baseline. Focusing on one domain and measuring before and after addresses most of these.

Is the IHL finding proof that AI causes higher growth?

No. It shows retailers using AI and machine learning grew faster, but those retailers may differ in other ways. Use it as a signal of where leaders invest, and test the benefit in your own business.
