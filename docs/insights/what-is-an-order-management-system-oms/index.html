What Is an Order Management System (OMS)? A Retailer's Guide

An order management system pulls orders from every channel into one place, keeps stock in sync and drives dispatch. How an OMS works and when you need one.6 min read6 cited sourcesUpdated 30 October 2026

Key figureAustralians spent $82.6 billion online in 2025, up 14% year on yearSource: auspost.com.au

An order management system (OMS) is the software that collects every order from every sales channel into one queue, keeps stock levels in step across those channels, and moves each order through picking, packing, labelling and dispatch. For a retailer selling on a web store plus marketplaces such as Amazon, eBay, Kogan or BIG W Market, the OMS becomes the operational centre: one place to see what was sold, what is left and what still needs to ship.

What an OMS actually does

At its simplest, an OMS replaces the habit of logging in to five seller portals each morning. The core jobs are:

  • Order capture. Orders arrive automatically from the web store and each marketplace, in a single format, with the channel, customer, items and promised dispatch date attached.
  • Inventory sync. When a unit sells on one channel, available stock drops on every other channel. This is what stops the same last unit selling twice.
  • Routing and allocation. If stock sits in more than one location, the OMS decides where each order ships from. Shopify describes the same idea in its own order routing rules: minimise split shipments, stay within the destination market and ship from the closest location.
  • Fulfilment workflow. Pick lists, packing checks, carrier selection, labels and manifests.
  • Status back to the channel. Tracking numbers and dispatch confirmations flow back to each marketplace so the customer sees progress and the seller's metrics stay healthy.
  • Returns and cancellations. Stock goes back into the available pool, and refunds are recorded against the original order.

An OMS is different from an ERP (which runs accounting, purchasing and the general ledger) and from a warehouse management system (which manages bin locations and labour inside a large warehouse). Many small and mid-sized online retailers need the OMS first, because their most pressing problem is coordinating channels, not running a distribution centre.

Why it matters more as channels multiply

Australian online retail is larger and more fragmented than it was a few years ago. Australia Post's eCommerce Report 2026 puts 2025 online spending at $82.6 billion, up 14% year on year, with 41% of households shopping online at least fortnightly. The same report notes households now shop across an average of 16 brands a year, so shoppers move between retailers and channels easily.

Marketplaces take a growing share of that spend. In 2024, online marketplaces attracted $16 billion, or 23% of all online spend, and drove 39% of online spend growth, according to the Australia Post eCommerce Report 2025. Australia Post also told the 2026 Online Retailer Conference that nearly four in ten parcels now come from global marketplaces, which have reset expectations for fast delivery, tracking and easy returns.

Each new channel adds a seller portal, a fee structure, a set of performance rules and another stock figure to maintain. Without a central system, the work grows roughly in line with the number of channels, and so does the risk of error.

The cost of running without one

Marketplaces measure sellers on operational performance, and the thresholds are tight. Amazon's Order Performance policy for Australia asks sellers to keep a pre-fulfilment cancellation rate under 2.5%, a late shipment rate under 4%, an order defect rate under 1% and a valid tracking rate above 95%. Cancellations are often caused by overselling, late shipments by orders sitting unseen in a portal, and missing tracking by manual label workflows. All three are symptoms an OMS is designed to remove.

There is also a strategic gap. Salesforce's Connected Shoppers research found 88% of retailers say unified commerce will be very important or critical to their success over the next two years, but only 15% have implemented a unified system and measured its results. Most retailers know they need one view of orders and stock; far fewer have built it.

Signs you have outgrown spreadsheets

You probably need an OMS if several of these are true:

  1. You sell on three or more channels, or plan to add a marketplace this year.
  2. Someone spends the first hour of each day downloading orders and updating stock.
  3. You have cancelled orders because stock was sold on two channels at once.
  4. Tracking numbers are typed or pasted back into marketplace portals.
  5. Peak season means overtime, temporary staff and a backlog.
  6. You cannot easily answer "how many units of this SKU can I sell right now?"

For a deeper look at the stock side of this, see inventory sync and stock accuracy across channels, and for the broader data question, why retail data belongs in a single source of truth.

What to look for when choosing an OMS

  • Native connections to your channels. Check the specific marketplaces you sell on or plan to, including Australian ones such as Woolworths Everyday Market, BIG W Market, Bunnings Marketplace, Kmart Marketplace, Myer Marketplace and Kogan.
  • Near real-time stock updates, with buffer rules per channel so you can hold back a few units where oversell penalties are harsh.
  • Carrier integration for the carriers you actually use, so labels, manifests and tracking are created in the same workflow.
  • Rules, not manual triage. Routing, carrier choice and priority flags should run automatically, with people handling exceptions.
  • Clean data model. One product record per SKU, mapped to each channel's listing, so changes are made once.
  • Reporting on dispatch times, cancellations and channel performance, so you can see marketplace metrics before they become warnings.

FAQ

Is an OMS the same as an inventory management system?

Not quite. Inventory management tracks how much stock you hold. An OMS uses that stock figure to accept, allocate and fulfil orders across channels, and pushes updated availability back to each channel. Most modern OMS platforms include inventory sync as a core feature.

Do small retailers need an OMS?

If you sell on one channel with low volume, a spreadsheet may be enough. Once you sell on several channels, the time spent re-keying orders and stock, plus the cost of cancellations and late shipments, usually justifies a system well before you reach large volumes.

Does an OMS replace my Shopify or WooCommerce store?

No. Your store remains a sales channel. The OMS sits behind it and your marketplaces, so orders, stock and dispatch are managed in one place regardless of where the sale happened.
