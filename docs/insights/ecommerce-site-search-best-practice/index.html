Site Search for Online Stores: Best Practice and Benchmarks

Why on-site search makes or breaks online sales, what benchmark research shows most stores get wrong, and the fixes Australian retailers should make first.5 min read5 cited sourcesUpdated 30 October 2026

Key figure56% of ecommerce sites fail to adequately support users' search needsSource: baymard.com

Site search is the box shoppers use when they already know what they want, which makes it one of the highest-intent tools on an online store. Yet Baymard Institute's 2026 benchmark finds 56% of ecommerce sites fail to adequately support users' search needs, and Google Cloud's research found that more than half of shoppers abandon their cart after an unsuccessful search. The fixes are mostly about understanding how people phrase queries and having clean product attributes to match them against.

Why site search deserves attention

Shoppers rely on it. In a Google Cloud-commissioned Harris Poll of nearly 13,500 adults in 14 countries (published 2023), US shoppers named the search box the most common way they look for products on retail sites (69%), and 88% said a good search function is very important or essential.

The stakes are clear on both sides:

  • After a successful search, 92% of US shoppers bought the item they searched for, and 78% bought at least one more item.
  • After an unsuccessful search, 53% (in the US and globally) said they typically abandon their cart and go elsewhere.
  • 76% of US shoppers had run a site search that did not return what they wanted, about four times in the month surveyed.

Google Cloud puts the cost of search abandonment at more than US$2 trillion a year globally. Whatever the exact figure, the direction is plain: search failures lose sales you had already won the attention for.

What most stores get wrong

Baymard tested how people actually search and benchmarked leading sites against its guidelines. Its search usability study of 19 leading ecommerce sites surfaced more than 700 search-specific usability issues, even on multi-million-dollar sites. Its 2026 query-type analysis found 46% of desktop sites, 58% of mobile sites and 64% of apps have "mediocre or worse" search performance. Failure rates by query type show where the gaps are:

Baymard also found that in its Accounts & Self-Service testing 34% of participants used the search field to look for non-product content such as return policies or order cancellation.

The pattern: stores handle exact names reasonably well, but fail on the descriptive, attribute-based queries real shoppers use. That is the same style of query people now type into AI assistants. Pew found that 53% of Google searches of ten words or more produced an AI summary, against 8% of one- or two-word searches. Shoppers are learning to describe what they want in full sentences and expect search to understand.

Fixes in priority order

  1. Clean, complete product attributes. Feature, use-case and compatibility searches only work if colour, material, size, compatible models and use cases exist as data on each product. This is the single biggest lever.
  2. Synonyms and Australian spelling. Map "pram" and "stroller", "doona" and "duvet", "thongs" and "flip-flops", "colour" and "color". Review your zero-result queries monthly and add synonyms.
  3. Handle units, symbols and abbreviations. Normalise "1/2 inch", "12.7mm" and "half inch"; "USB C" and "USB-C".
  4. Return content results. Let searches for "returns", "delivery times" or "afterpay" surface the relevant help page.
  5. Never show a dead end. On zero results, suggest close matches, categories and popular products.
  6. Autocomplete that helps. Suggest categories and query refinements, not just product names, and keep it fast on mobile.
  7. Filters that match the query. If someone searches "women's waterproof jacket", pre-apply relevant filters and show sizes in stock.
  8. Use search data. Your search logs are a free list of demand. Zero-result queries reveal missing products, missing attributes or missing synonyms.

Natural-language and AI-assisted search

AI-assisted site search can interpret descriptive queries ("quiet fan for a bedroom") and map them to attributes. It still depends on the underlying data: an AI layer cannot match "quiet" if no product records a noise level. Salesforce's 2026 ANZ research found 42% of Australian commerce organisations are optimising product descriptions for natural language, which benefits both site search and external AI discovery. FAQ

What is a good site search conversion rate?

It varies by category and store, so compare against your own non-search visitors instead of a generic benchmark. Searchers should convert noticeably better because they show clear intent; if they do not, search is likely failing them.

How often should I review search terms?

Monthly at minimum, and weekly during peak periods. Focus on zero-result and low-click queries.

Do I need an AI search tool?

Not always. Many gains come from attributes, synonyms and zero-result handling. AI search adds most value for large catalogues and descriptive queries, once the data is in good shape.
