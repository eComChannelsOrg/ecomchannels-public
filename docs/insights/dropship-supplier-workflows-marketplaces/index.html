Dropship and Supplier Workflows: Run Them Without the Chaos

Dropshipping widens your range without holding stock, but marketplaces hold you responsible. How to run supplier and dropship workflows that meet the rules.5 min read7 cited sourcesUpdated 30 October 2026

Key figureTemple & Webster sells 200,000+ products from hundreds of suppliers via drop-shipSource: templeandwebstergroup.com.au

A dropship workflow lets you sell products a supplier ships directly to your customer, so you can widen your range without holding the stock. It only works if supplier stock feeds your listings automatically, orders reach the supplier immediately, tracking comes back to you before the marketplace deadline, and the customer sees you as the seller throughout. Amazon and eBay both allow drop shipping only under conditions, and you remain responsible for the customer experience and for consumer guarantees.

Why retailers use dropship and supplier fulfilment

Dropshipping turns range into a data problem instead of a capital problem. You list more products without buying, storing or insuring them. In Australia the best-known example is Temple & Webster, which describes an innovative drop-shipping model where products are sent directly to customers by hundreds of local suppliers, with more than 200,000 products on sale. The company says the model enables faster delivery and reduces the need to hold inventory.

For smaller retailers, common patterns include:

  • Full dropship. A supplier holds stock and ships every order for certain product lines.
  • Hybrid. Fast sellers are held in your warehouse; long-tail or bulky items ship from the supplier.
  • Third-party fulfilment. A 3PL holds your own stock and ships on your behalf.
  • Selling as a dropship supplier. You ship orders for a retailer or marketplace that lists your range.

Each one adds a party between the order and the parcel, which is where workflows break down.

What the marketplaces require

Marketplaces allow dropshipping only if the customer experience is the same as if you shipped it yourself.

Amazon. Amazon's Australian Drop Shipping Policy says drop shipping is not acceptable unless it is clear to the customer that you are the seller of record. You must have an agreement where you are the only seller identified on packing slips, invoices and external packaging; remove any other seller's paperwork before shipping; and remain solely responsible for accepting and processing returns. Buying from another retailer and having it shipped direct to your customer is prohibited if it identifies anyone else.

eBay. eBay Australia's third-party fulfilment policy allows dropshipping only when certain conditions are met. It is not allowed if packaging makes it look as though the item was bought from another retailer or marketplace, or if the buyer's personal data is not sufficiently protected. Listing an item and then buying it from another retailer that ships direct is not allowed.

Performance metrics still apply. Dropshipped orders count towards your metrics. Amazon's Order Performance policy requires a late shipment rate under 4%, pre-fulfilment cancellations under 2.5% and valid tracking above 95%. eBay's seller standards cap transaction defects at 2%. A slow supplier is your late shipment.

Consumer law applies to you. Under the Australian Consumer Law, the ACCC notes that products must be of acceptable quality and match the description given by the business. Using a supplier to fulfil an order does not move those obligations off the seller. This is general information, not legal advice.

Where dropship workflows break

  • Stale stock feeds. The supplier sells out but your listing stays live, producing cancellations.
  • Slow order hand-off. Orders are emailed in a batch at the end of the day and miss dispatch windows.
  • Missing tracking. The supplier ships but the tracking number arrives late or not at all.
  • Wrong paperwork. A supplier invoice or branded carton reaches your customer, breaching marketplace policy.
  • Product data gaps. Supplier descriptions are thin or inconsistent, leading to "not as described" returns.
  • Returns confusion. The customer does not know where to send an item back, and the supplier does not know to expect it.

Delivery is where shoppers judge you. Australia Post's eCommerce Report 2026 found three in four shoppers say a good delivery experience makes them shop online more.

Building a workflow that holds up

  1. Agree service levels in writing: dispatch time, cut-off, tracking turnaround, packaging rules, returns process and stock feed frequency.
  2. Automate the stock feed. Import supplier availability on a schedule and push it to every channel, with a buffer for suppliers that update less often.
  3. Route orders automatically. Dropship SKUs should go to the right supplier the moment the order lands, with your branding on any paperwork.
  4. Collect tracking by integration or portal, and push it to the marketplace automatically.
  5. Set handling times per SKU that reflect each supplier's real performance.
  6. Own the product data. Rewrite supplier content into your own master record that meets each marketplace's rules.
  7. Score suppliers monthly on dispatch time, cancellations, damage and returns.

FAQ

Is dropshipping allowed on Amazon and eBay in Australia?

Yes, with conditions. Both require that the customer sees you as the seller, and both prohibit buying from another retailer to ship direct. You remain responsible for returns and performance metrics.

Who is responsible if a dropshipped product is faulty?

The customer deals with you as the seller, and consumer guarantees apply to the sale. Your supplier agreement should set out how faults, returns and costs are handled between you. Seek legal advice for your situation.

How often should supplier stock update?

As often as the supplier can provide it, ideally several times a day. For suppliers that update daily or less, use a stock buffer to avoid overselling.
